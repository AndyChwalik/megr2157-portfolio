#About Me

My name is Andy Chwalik. I am 20 years old, and I am a transfer student from Appalachian State University. I am pursuing my bachelor's degree in mechanical engineering and considering dual majoring in either physics or mathematics. My interest in engineering began in high school through the PLTW program, showing me the engineering process and how to collaborate in a team effectively. My favorite classes from the PLTW program are Aerospace Engineering and Principles of Engineering. In these classes, we discussed the basic principles of fluid motion and how those forces interact with and influence solid objects, which sparked my interest in working with fluid-related systems.

Because of that, I want to have a concentration in either aerospace or marine engineering. Both fields appeal to me due to the sheer scale of the projects they work on and the complexity in designing and maintaining the machines. They also both deal with objects moving through large bodies of fluids.

To explore aerospace engineering, I visited GE Aerospace, and airplane engine manufacturer. I liked their system of different independent sub teams working on different engines. The feature that stood out to me the most were these channels behind the fan blade. From my memory, the channels compress the air coming into the engine in order to generate more thrust and improve fuel efficiency. I also visited the aerospace club at UNC Charlotte; however, I wasn’t very interested in staying because the teams seemed really large for the amount of work being completed. I haven’t been able to explore marine engineering as much as aerospace engineering, but I am still intrigued by the size of the projects and relation to fluid dynamics.

"What does it mean to defend an engineering decision : and do you currently know how to do it?"

I think defending an engineering decision requires a lot of brainstorming, calculations, data, and testing. Brainstorming is included in this process due to the vast amount of ideas that can come from a simple prompt. I would be really surprised if professional engineers picked what they thought was the best idea, and it worked 100% of the time. So, if an idea fails, there is a list of other great ideas to choose from because of the brainstorming process.

Moving along, the individual calculations are very important to make sure that the idea doesn't break/explode upon testing. In my head, this section takes the longest in the engineering process due to all of the different parts at play. 

Lastly, testing is crucial to make sure that everything works in the real world. Even if the idea seems perfect and the calculations are all correct, it is all in theory. The actual performance of an idea will not be known until it is extensively tested in the areas it is designed for. If a design fails, you'll have to go through the entire process of trying to defend your engineering decision again.

Based on my description, I think I know how to defend an engineering decision; and, this has been put to practice in our freshman engineering classes. In 1300, we had to come up with what we thought was the best idea for a grand challenge given to us. To do so, we had to go through this whole process extensively.
